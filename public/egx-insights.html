How a stock gets scored

Nine components.
Six starts the deep dive.

Every candidate is scored the same way, every run. Five components add points for evidence; four subtract them for the ways a setup is already too late or too risky. The score is a research-ranking aid — it is not a buy signal. A high score means the situation deserves a closer look, nothing more.

  • Fresh, specific, material disclosurea real filing, not a rumour or a press mention+4
  • Economic importance to the companyis the news big relative to this company's size?+3
  • Volume and early price confirmationRV20 — volume against its own 20-session median+2
  • Repeat-buyer or ownership clusterthe same holder building, disclosure after disclosure+2
  • A clear dated catalyst aheadsomething on the calendar, not a vague hope+2
  • Already up more than 20% in five sessionsthe anti-chasing penalty−4
  • Company denies knowing why it movedtold the exchange it has no explanation−4
  • Two or more completed limit-upsEGP's ±20% band already used up−3
  • Severe valuation, dilution or exit riskincluding a float too thin to sell into−3
−40+4
+13best possible score
+6the research line — confirmation still required
−14worst possible score

The penalties can outweigh everything. A stock can score full marks on all five positives and still fail, because the four penalties are worth more (−14) than the five positives are (+13). That is deliberate: the scanner is built to find things before they move, so a name that has already run is penalised harder than good news can reward it. Most days nothing qualifies — and that is the scanner working, not failing.

Scoring rules are fixed in the research playbook and are not changed to fit a result. Nothing here is investment advice or a prediction — every signal is tracked afterwards in the marked-to-market ledger below, including the ones that went wrong.
